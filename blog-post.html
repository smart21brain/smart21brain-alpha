How to Help Children Love Learning

Faith Njoroge Jul 28, 2026 5 min read

Every parent has watched a child light up over a topic they weren't "supposed" to be interested in yet — dinosaurs, space, a video game's backstory. That spark is the whole game. Our job isn't to manufacture curiosity; it's to stop getting in its way.

1. Follow the question, not the curriculum

When a child asks "why is the sky blue," that's not a detour from learning — it is learning. Answering with genuine curiosity (even a "let's find out together") teaches them that questions are welcome, not an interruption.

2. Let them see you struggle productively

Kids who only see adults being competent assume struggle means failure. Narrate your own problem-solving out loud — even something as small as figuring out a recipe — so they see that getting it wrong is just part of getting it right.

3. Protect unstructured time

Between school, homework and activities, many children have almost no time left to be bored — and boredom is where a lot of self-directed learning starts. Leaving gaps in the schedule isn't wasted time.

4. Make screens a tool, not a babysitter

The difference isn't screen time versus no screen time — it's passive scrolling versus active, guided exploration. A well-chosen video lesson or game, discussed afterward, does more than an hour of unrelated content.

5. Celebrate effort, not just results

"You worked really hard on that" builds a different relationship with learning than "you're so smart." One says effort pays off; the other says ability is fixed. Over years, that difference compounds.

Faith Njoroge
Product Design, Smart21Brain
