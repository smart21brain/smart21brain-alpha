    the electron

    Meet the electron

    Every circuit, chip and screen in this lab runs on one tiny particle. Here is what it is, how it behaves in a wire, and how it grew into the computer in your pocket.

    Charge−1.602 × 10−19 CThe smallest free charge in everyday electronics. Symbol e⁻.
    Mass9.109 × 10−31 kgAbout 1⁄1836 of a proton, so it is easy to push around.
    Discovered1897By J. J. Thomson, while studying cathode rays at Cambridge.
    In one ampere6.24 × 1018 per secondThat many electrons pass any point of the wire every second.
    Fundamental particleSpin ½, no known partsIt is a lepton: not made of anything smaller that we have found.
    Free electrons in copper8.5 × 1028 per m³About one free electron per copper atom, which is why copper conducts so well.

    Why it matters in electronics

    • Conductors (copper, silver, gold) have loosely held outer electrons that move freely.
    • Insulators (rubber, glass, plastic) hold their electrons tightly, so current cannot flow.
    • Semiconductors (silicon has 4 outer electrons) can be switched between the two. That is how a transistor works.
    • Conventional current is drawn from + to −, the opposite way to the electrons. Benjamin Franklin chose it more than a century before the electron was found, and every circuit diagram still uses it.
    • Electron volt (eV) is the energy of one electron moved through 1 volt: 1.602 × 10−19 J. Silicon needs about 1.12 eV to free an electron.

    From one electron to your phone

    1. 1897The electron is discoveredJ. J. Thomson shows that cathode rays are streams of tiny negative particles.
    2. 1904The vacuum tubeJohn Ambrose Fleming invents the diode, the first device that controls the flow of electrons.
    3. 1947The transistorBardeen, Brattain and Shockley build it at Bell Labs: a tiny switch with no glass tube and no hot filament.
    4. 1958The integrated circuitJack Kilby puts several parts on one piece of semiconductor at Texas Instruments.
    5. 1971The microprocessorIntel's 4004 packs about 2,300 transistors onto one chip. It was a 4-bit CPU, a small cousin of the 8-bit one in the CPU Emulator.
    6. TodayTens of billionsA modern processor holds tens of billions of transistors, each one switching electrons billions of times a second.

    Data science workflow
    1. Ask a clear question.
    2. Collect data, with permission.
    3. Clean: fix missing values, duplicates, units.
    4. Explore: summary stats and charts.
    5. Model: start simple (average, then regression).
    6. Validate on data the model has not seen.
    7. Communicate findings and their limits.
    Engineering design process
    1. Define the problem and constraints (cost, time, safety).
    2. Research existing solutions.
    3. Brainstorm several ideas, then choose one.
    4. Prototype a small version.
    5. Test against clear measurements.
    6. Improve and repeat. Iteration is the method.
    Lab safety rules
    • Use batteries or low-voltage USB (5 V). Never experiment with mains electricity.
    • Work with an adult when using a soldering iron, hot glue or tools.
    • Never short-circuit a battery, especially lithium cells.
    • Always add a resistor in series with an LED.
    • Disconnect power before changing wiring.
